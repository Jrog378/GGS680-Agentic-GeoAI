## 1. Algorithm, step by step
1. **Model** (eqs. 1–4, p. 3): radiance `L = k1·r + k2` (k1: solar irradiance, sky radiance, transmission; k2: path radiance) and `DC = k3·L + k4`, so per band i and date d: `DC_di = m_di·r_di + b_di`.
2. **PIF assumption** (eq. 5, p. 4): for objects with `r_1i = r_2i`, Day-1 and Day-2 counts are linearly related. PIFs are "typically man-made objects whose reflectivity … is nearly constant for the imaging conditions and which are independent of seasonal or biological cycles. Examples are road surfaces, rooftops, parking lots" (p. 4).
3. **Transform from PIF histogram statistics** (eqs. 6–9, p. 4):
   `DC_1i = (σ_1i/σ_2i)·DC_2i + DC̄_1i − (σ_1i/σ_2i)·DC̄_2i`, i.e. `m_i = σ_1i/σ_2i`, `b_i = DC̄_1i − m_i·DC̄_2i` from the PIF means and SDs on each date. Applied to the **entire** Day-2 image via a lookup table (p. 6).
4. **PIF segmentation** (pp. 4–6, Fig. 1): (a) NIR/red ratio (TM 4/3) thresholded interactively from 255 downward until only "water" and "urban" are ON (vegetation OFF); (b) TM 7 (SWIR) thresholded from 0 upward so water and wet farmland are OFF; (c) PIF mask = (a) AND (b) → urban features only. Done separately for each date. Mean and SD per band computed over mask pixels (zero bin discarded).
5. **Reference image:** "the image with the largest dynamic range is generally selected as the reference image (Day 1)" (p. 7).
6. **Segmentation check** (eq. 10, pp. 7–8): also compute a histogram-matching transform of the PIF histograms; compare with the linear transform over the input DC range covering the central 90% of PIFs (DC5–DC95): `ε_T = sqrt(Σ (DC_i − DC'_i)² / n)`. Accept the segmentation if the average difference is **< 2 digital counts in all bands**; else the mask is too small or has too many mixed pixels.
7. **Accuracy** (eqs. 11–15, pp. 8–9): raw RMS on control targets after transform; sampling error from regressing Day-1 on Day-2 target counts (eq. 13); PIF error `ε_PIF = sqrt(ε² − ε²_samp)` (eq. 14); converted to reflectance via an in-scene DC-to-reflectance regression (eq. 15).

## 2. Inputs, outputs, data needs
- Input: two co-registered multispectral images; segmentation needs **NIR, red and SWIR** (TM 3, 4, 7) or IR+red for the CIR air photo.
- Data in the paper: Landsat TM Rochester NY (13 Sep 1982, 22 Jun 1984 reference, 24 May 1985), urban and rural 512 × 512 subscenes, a simulated 60 m version; NHAP CIR air photo of Buffalo (7 Jun 1970 → 6 Jul 1972), digitised to ~10 m, 512 × 512 (pp. 10–12).
- Output: per-band gain/offset (lookup table) mapping Day 2 to Day 1.

## 3. Quantitative results
- Linear vs histogram-matching transforms differ "typically less than a digital count out to the near-infrared and slightly higher for longer wavelengths" (p. 12; Table 1: 0.30–2.78 DC). "Nearly identical solutions are generated by the two methods" (p. 8). Authors then used the linear form "exclusively because of its theoretically sound basis, its simplicity … and its empirical similarity" to histogram matching (p. 12).
- Urban Rochester control points (Table 2, p. 12): untransformed 1.5–37.5 DC; after transform 1.7–9.5 DC; after sampling correction ≈ 0.4–1.6 %R (bands 1–4). "of the order of 1 reflectance unit" (p. 13).
- Buffalo air photo (Table 3): after correction 0.42 %R (red), 0.62 (green), 2.18 (IR).
- Robustness: rural vs urban transforms differ 1.4–9.6 DC (1982, 7,686 PIF px) and 1.4–3.2 DC (1985, 39,191 PIF px) (Table 4) — about 1 reflectance unit; 30 m vs 60 m transforms differ 0.20–1.81 DC (Table 5), "little or no loss in effectiveness" over 2:1 resolution (p. 14).

## 4. Stated limitations (p. 14)
- "the segmentation process presented here was highly interpreter dependent"; "no attempt was made … to optimize results".
- **Moisture:** "Major changes in surface moisture will change the overall reflectivity of the features that were assumed to be invariant. To avoid this problem, the transformation should only be attempted when the impervious surfaces that predominate the PIF population can be assumed to be reasonably dry … surface moisture can remain present for several days following any sort of precipitation."
- One global linear transform per band (first-order illumination, atmosphere, sensor).

## 5. How to apply to our pipeline
- **Directly relevant to our imagery:** Schott's PIFs are *roads, rooftops and parking lots*, the very objects SpaceNet 8 labels. But the post-storm date breaks his explicit dryness condition: after a hurricane, roads and roofs are wet (or flooded) for days. So a PIF fit on roads of the after date will absorb wet-surface darkening as if it were atmosphere. Choose PIFs on the after date that are **not** flood-labelled and preferably elevated (roofs), and expect a residual from wetness; report it.
- **No NIR/SWIR:** the paper's ratio-based PIF mask is unavailable. Substitute: SN8 building/road footprints (labelled, on the before date) minus any flood-labelled or cloud-gated pixels, minus a two-date difference outlier test (Du 2002 / IR-MAD style). Note that using SN8 labels to choose fitting pixels must stay within the labelled-data rules (labels used only as masks, unmodified).
- **Tone curve choice:** the paper supports our quantile (histogram) matching only as a *diagnostic* and found it ≈ linear on PIFs; the published form is linear mean/SD matching (eq. 8). Arm "Schott-linear": m = σ_before/σ_after, b = μ_before − m·μ_after over PIF pixels, per band; QC = ε_T between this line and our quantile curve over the PIF 5–95% range, accept if < 2 DC (paper's threshold, 8-bit TM; our 8-bit RGB is the same scale).
- **Reference date:** paper picks the largest dynamic range; ours is fixed (the clear before date), which is fine as both are relative.

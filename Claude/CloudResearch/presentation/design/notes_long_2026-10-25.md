# Speaker notes, long version (2026-10-25, before the cut to about 4 minutes)

## cover
We are building an agentic change detection system for satellite images taken after disasters. One difficulty we have run into is clouds in our electro-optical imagery: before the system can say what changed, it has to decide whether it can see the ground at all. I will show our proposed method of study, with the research questions and the literature behind each step, then the results so far, as pictures and as numbers. Everything here is preliminary and on development data.

## question
We are building an agentic change detection system: a vision foundation model finds where two satellite images differ, an agent describes what changed, and code checks every fact. One difficulty we have run into is clouds in our electro-optical imagery. In my own words: clouds hide parts of the after image, and the human labels are shaped by the same cloud. That gives three research questions, which we will work on in whatever order the work allows. Reporting: what can a map reliably say about roads it cannot see. Handling: whether the after image alone does better when only the before image is cloudy. Detection: whether synthetic clouds behave like real ones, because they are our test bed. The working definition, which I am still settling, is that change means change on the ground, so a cloud is something to see through or report, not a change.

## workflow
Three stages. Detect uses classical greyscale masks from GGS 680: bright cloud cores, the dark channel, and how much the dark channel rises from the before image to the after image. Handle is the agent gate from GGS 662: for each feature it decides to assess it, correct it with the second image and run the detector again, or say cannot assess. Report gives every statement about a hidden feature a tier: reliable, synthetic only, or a range. The detector underneath is a random forest on classical, texture and DINOv2 cues. The agents propose questions and critiques; I make the decisions.

## data
The data are SpaceNet 8's Louisiana-East scenes from Hurricane Ida: Maxar images before and after the storm at half a metre, used in greyscale. Each outline is a development tile coloured by its cloud: 17 tiles are under opaque cloud and many more under thin cloud or haze. The roads come from OpenStreetMap with SpaceNet 8's flood labels. Two checks matter: the flood label is either yes or missing, never no, and the split's fingerprint is logged, so the test tiles, the north negative set and the other areas have never been scored; every slip is logged in the data ledger. For cloud itself I use HRC_WHU, a set of expert cloud masks, with its test images sealed too.

## result1
The first result is about the labels, not the model. SpaceNet 8's analysts were told to only attribute what was visible, and the flood label is yes or missing, never no. So a road under cloud reads as dry. On clear roads 55% are labelled flooded; under thin cloud only 26%, and on opaque-cloud tiles 2%. Part of that gap is geography, but on this tile every flooded label sits on the clear side. Scoring a model on these labels rewards it for calling cloud dry. And under thin cloud an earlier fit of the model found 67% of the flooded roads against 87% in clear sky. I checked the cloud mask with synthetic haze: under thin haze it does not miss flooded roads more than dry ones.

## result2
This is the two-date idea, on the road with the most metres under thin cloud. The before image tells us how dark the ground's darkest pixels should be, so how much they brightened gives the haze, and we only correct inside the cloud mask. The baseline is single-image de-hazing from He and colleagues, which has to assume the darkest pixel is black. On synthetic haze over 94 clear tiles, where we know the answer, single-image de-hazing gets closer on brightness, but it changes 85% of the clear ground and keeps less detail; ours leaves clear ground alone and keeps the detail. That haze follows our model, so the check favours us, and faint haze outside the cloud mask is left alone. On this tile the haze lifts but little road detail comes back, and the thick core stays opaque: cannot assess. The flood calls on the de-hazed images are on the next slide.

## flood
Then the flood calls, with each run's plan written down before it ran. With the model trained on the images as they are, two-date de-hazing finds 20 of the 24 flooded roads under thin cloud against 17, but it costs the clear roads on those tiles, 78 down to 72 percent right. When the model is also trained on de-hazed images, it still finds 20, and the clear roads are back to about 77 percent: the drop came from training on one kind of image and testing on another. It still calls 30 of the 69 dry-labelled cloudy roads flooded; those labels cannot be trusted, so we cannot yet say how many are wrong. Adding cloud as an extra variable to the random forest found no more flooded roads; it cut those calls, but so did training on cloudy roads without it, which is the model learning that cloudy roads were labelled dry. Next is the clean test: train on de-hazed images, then test once on unseen roads.

## next
The pieces all run: the data, the masks, the de-hazer and the held-out test harness, and the cloud mask beats the old rule against human cloud labels. The limits are real: one event, one sensor, roads only, all the real cloud in one after image, and only 24 trusted flooded roads under thin cloud. The methodology critic's verdict was revise, and those changes are in the next steps: pre-register first, build and check the synthetic test bed, test before-image cloud, set the report tiers, then one look at the sealed data. I'll end with a question for you: when a map cannot see the ground, should it say cannot assess, show a range, or fill it in from another date?

## masks
This is the check on the cloud masks against human cloud labels, HRC_WHU's expert masks on 120 development images. The old bright rule is precise but finds only about a quarter of the cloud; the dark-channel mask finds about two thirds with F1 0.65. It is best on vegetation and water and its false alarms are bright bare ground. The threshold learned on SpaceNet 8 is close to the best one on HRC_WHU. I also checked whether the mask hides floods selectively, using synthetic haze on clear roads: under thin haze it does not; under thick haze it flags flooded roads more, not less.

## layering
This is how the two images are layered. The haze model says a hazy pixel is the ground dimmed by t, plus the airlight A times one minus t. The dark channel, the darkest pixel in a 20 metre square, is the key: under cloud even the darkest pixel brightens. The single-image method assumes the ground's darkest pixel is black. We have the before image, so we know how dark it really was, once the two dates' grey levels are matched with a curve on clear pixels. How much it brightened gives t. We only do this inside the cloud mask, fading out over 20 metres past it, and only for cloud blobs of at least 2,500 square metres, so a changed roof is not read as haze. Where t drops below 0.2 the ground cannot be recovered, so that part is cannot assess.
